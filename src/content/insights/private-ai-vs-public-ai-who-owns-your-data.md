## "I can just use ChatGPT or Claude for that."

That's what a lot of business owners say when the subject of AI automation comes up. And they're not wrong. Public AI models are genuinely capable. For a huge range of tasks, they work well, they're fast to start using, and they require no setup.

But there's a question most businesses aren't asking: what are they giving up in exchange for that convenience?

Think about what actually gets fed into these tools every day, inside most companies. Client contracts. Financial statements. Employee records. Customer databases. Internal reports. Business strategy documents. All of it typed, pasted, or uploaded into a platform owned and operated by someone else.

![A meme reacting to the assumption that public AI tools like Claude handle sensitive business data safely by default.](/insights/images/claude-does-that-for-me-meme.jpg)

## What businesses are really trading

Public AI companies have a clear incentive: they want to become the operating layer underneath as many businesses as possible. The more a company's employees rely on their models, the more its workflows run through their platforms, and the more its data becomes part of that ecosystem, the harder that company becomes to leave.

Today, the product is useful and the terms feel generous. That's not a guarantee about tomorrow. Pricing changes. Usage limits change. Data retention policies change. Terms of service change. And a business that has quietly built its daily operations around a platform it doesn't control has very little say when any of that happens.

This isn't a new pattern. It's the same shape seen with other technology platforms before AI: convenience gets a business hooked, dependency follows, and the platform holder ends up with most of the leverage in the relationship.

## Why privacy tools aren't a full answer

To be fair, privacy-preserving techniques and anonymisation tools do help, and they're worth using. But they aren't a complete shield. AI models can sometimes infer sensitive information from details that look harmless on their own, stitched together from context rather than from any single explicit data point.

The more fundamental issue doesn't go away with better anonymisation: a business cannot build real data sovereignty on top of infrastructure it doesn't own or control.

## A different approach: private, fine-tuned models

For the internal systems built for clients, Kalvron uses a different setup: private AI models, fine-tuned and configured around a business's specific workflows, running inside the company's own infrastructure rather than sending sensitive records out to a public AI platform.

The goal of this approach is straightforward:

- The business's data stays under its own control.
- The AI is configured around that business's actual operations, not a generic use case.
- Day-to-day workflows don't depend entirely on a third-party AI platform's continued goodwill.
- The business gets the practical benefits of AI without handing over control of its own data as the price of admission.

## This isn't an argument against public AI models

Public AI models are genuinely powerful, and this isn't a case against using them. The argument is narrower than that: businesses need to stop confusing *access* to powerful AI with *ownership* of their AI infrastructure. Those are two very different things, and the gap between them matters more as a business builds more of its operations around AI over time.

An AI system should make a business more capable. It shouldn't make that business permanently dependent on whoever happens to be providing the intelligence underneath it.

## Where this leaves a business owner

The practical takeaway isn't "never use public AI tools." It's knowing which workloads are fine to run through a public model, and which ones, the sensitive, operationally critical ones, deserve a system that a business actually owns and controls.

A company's intelligence layer, the systems that know its clients, its finances, its operations, is worth owning outright rather than renting indefinitely from a provider whose incentives won't always point the same direction as the business's own.
